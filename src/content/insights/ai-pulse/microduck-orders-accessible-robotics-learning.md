## What happened

Pollen Robotics introduced Microduck on 27 August 2026: a small biped robot offered for pre-order at $399 before taxes and shipping, with software for experimenting with movement and training.

The following day, Business Insider reported Hugging Face cofounder Thomas Wolf's statement that customers had ordered more than $2.6 million of Microducks in the first 24 hours.

That figure was a company representative's claim about orders. It was not evidence of completed deliveries, sustained customer use or a profitable deployment. The more interesting signal is the appetite for a tangible way to explore robotics.

## Why it matters

A small physical system can make an abstract technology easier to investigate. People can see what happens when a task is unclear, a movement fails or a change produces an unexpected result.

For an organisation considering robotics education, that accessibility is useful only if the learning objective is clear. Buying several appealing devices does not automatically create technical capability.

I would decide what participants should be able to explain afterwards. Perhaps they should distinguish a demonstration from a repeatable behaviour, describe the role of a simulator or design a simple evaluation. Those outcomes determine whether the hardware is appropriate and what support the session needs.

The equipment budget should also include preparation, troubleshooting and supervision. A low entry price can reduce one barrier while leaving others unchanged.

## The bigger shift

There is room for a staged approach to learning about physical AI. Start with observation and simulation, move to a controlled demonstration, then consider a narrow practical exercise. Each stage should answer a different question.

An illustrative workshop might ask participants to describe a behaviour, predict where it will fail and compare that prediction with observed results. They could record unsuccessful attempts as carefully as successful ones. A final discussion would connect the exercise to the operating conditions of their own business.

That is more informative than presenting a successful trick as evidence that a machine is ready for unrelated tasks.

Keep the distinction between educational exploration and workplace deployment explicit. A device suitable for learning does not necessarily meet the requirements of a production environment. Reliability, support, safety and integration would need their own assessment.

When planning the wider programme, the guide to [corporate AI training costs](/insights/executive-education/corporate-ai-training-cost-dubai/) helps separate equipment from the people and preparation needed to make learning useful.

## My take

An accessible robot can earn attention in a way that another presentation cannot. I would use that attention to develop better questions about automation.

Ask participants what the machine is actually doing, what information it receives and what makes a result repeatable. Have them identify which part of the demonstration depends on preparation that an audience might not see.

Then ask them to propose a realistic business evaluation, including a reason to reject the technology if it does not fit the job. Curiosity should lead to stronger judgement, not an obligation to buy.

The early order figure is a promising sign of interest. The lasting value will depend on what people learn and build after the initial excitement. For a company, the right measure is capability gained, rather than the number of devices placed on desks.
