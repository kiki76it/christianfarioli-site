A customer does not always begin shopping with a search. Sometimes the intention sits quietly in the background: the next book from a favourite author, a new device in a familiar range, or tickets for an artist they follow. An assistant that remembers that intention can influence when shopping begins.

## What happened

In a 1 September 2026 update, Amazon described Alexa's Update Me When feature. A user can ask the assistant to watch for relevant developments, including product releases and entertainment announcements. Alexa checks Amazon and the wider web and sends a notification when it finds an update. The instruction comes from the user. This is distinct from permission to buy something. [Amazon explains the feature here](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/how-to-use-amazon-shopping-ai-assistant).

The commercial implication is worth examining without exaggerating the capability. An alert about a new product is an invitation to consider it. It does not establish that the recommendation is impartial, that the product is suitable, or that a sale will follow.

## Why it matters

For a brand, this suggests a different question from how to win a single search result: what useful change would justify contacting someone who has already expressed an interest?

Consider a specialist equipment supplier. A notification that a compatible replacement part is available could solve a real problem. An alert for every minor colour change could make the same service irritating. The distinction comes from the customer's purpose, not the company's desire to generate another visit.

I would start with three inputs: a clearly described product, a reliable availability date and an understandable reason the change matters. If a retailer cannot maintain those facts consistently, an AI assistant merely provides another route for distributing the inconsistency.

Marketing teams should also decide how they would measure usefulness. Clicks reveal attention, but complaints, repeated dismissals and whether the customer found the promised item help explain the quality of that attention. A burst of visits to an unavailable product is a poor result, however impressive the notification statistics look.

## The bigger shift

My reading is that this kind of feature moves part of discovery from an isolated query into a continuing relationship. That relationship needs boundaries.

A customer may want one update when a product launches, rather than a permanent stream of related offers. They may care about a precise model rather than an entire category. Their interest may expire after they buy elsewhere. These are design questions a business should resolve before describing persistent attention as personalisation.

There is also a planning implication. Product information, customer service and marketing cannot give an assistant three different accounts of what is available. Keeping those accounts consistent is less glamorous than generating a campaign, but it creates the conditions for a useful recommendation.

This is an example of the broader argument in [why AI strategy beats AI tools](/insights/ai-strategy/why-ai-strategy-beats-ai-tools/): select the customer problem and the operating requirements before choosing the interface.

## My take

I see value in an assistant that remembers something I deliberately asked it to watch. The value depends on how faithfully it preserves that intention.

For companies, the next step should be small. Choose one product family and describe the specific events customers would reasonably want to hear about. Check the underlying information, define when the interest expires and agree how a person can stop the updates.

Then evaluate whether the alert helped a customer make a better decision. The opportunity is to become relevant at the right moment. Earning that moment requires restraint as well as technical capability.
