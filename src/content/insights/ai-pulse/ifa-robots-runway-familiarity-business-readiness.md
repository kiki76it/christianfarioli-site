A robot on a runway is easy to remember. A robot that quietly completes a useful task for several months is harder to demonstrate in a short video. Businesses need to keep both forms of attention in perspective when evaluating embodied AI.

## What happened

IFA Berlin's programme scheduled Robots on the Runway for 5 September 2026. Humanoid machines, quadrupeds and companion robots were presented in a fashion-show format. Dated on-site photography records walking, posing and other demonstrations at the event. The image report is supplied by ZUMA Press Wire through Reuters Connect. [The organiser's programme](https://www.ifa-berlin.com/programme/robots-on-the-runway) and [the dated report](https://www.reutersconnect.com/item/germany-humanoid-robots-take-the-runway-at-ifa-2026-in-berlin/dGFnOnJldXRlcnMuY29tLDIwMjY6bmV3c21sX01UMVpVTUEwMDBKNzVCSEs) establish the showcase.

A curated performance demonstrates what was shown under those conditions. It does not establish how a machine will cope with a crowded shop, an unfamiliar layout or a long working day.

## Why it matters

My interpretation is that events like this can make robots feel less remote. A familiar public format helps people imagine machines outside a laboratory. That may open conversations which a technical specification would not.

But familiarity can also make a buying decision feel more mature than it is. A manager may see an approachable machine and assume the remaining challenge is simply choosing where to put it.

Consider a proposed visitor assistant in a showroom. Before discussing its personality or appearance, the business should define the service. Does it help someone find a department, answer an availability question or contact a colleague? What happens when the visitor cannot hear it, uses an unexpected language or asks about a complaint?

Those details determine whether the robot contributes useful capacity or creates work for staff who must repeatedly rescue the interaction. Neither outcome can be inferred from a stage performance.

## The bigger shift

Embodied AI places a digital interface into a shared physical space. That adds operational questions which a browser-based demonstration can conceal.

A trial should examine where the machine waits, how it behaves around people and who is responsible when it becomes unavailable. It should also establish an accessible alternative for visitors who do not want to interact with it. These are proposed evaluation requirements, not claims about the systems shown at IFA.

I would measure a pilot against the service it replaces or supplements. Useful measures might include completed visitor requests, staff interventions, waiting time and incidents requiring a pause. Maintenance and supervision belong in the cost calculation alongside purchase or rental fees.

The business also needs to distinguish attraction from service. A robot could be effective as an event experience without being suitable for unattended customer support. That can still be a legitimate objective, provided the company describes and measures it honestly.

This is another reason to start with [the business purpose of AI](/insights/ai-strategy/why-ai-strategy-beats-ai-tools/) before selecting an eye-catching interface.

## My take

The runway is a reasonable place to create curiosity. A deployment decision needs a different setting.

I would invite interested teams to define one narrow job and test it in the environment where it will actually happen, with staff able to intervene. Include ordinary interruptions, unclear requests and periods when the machine is unavailable.

Then ask the people using and supporting it whether the service improved. If the strongest result is that visitors enjoyed seeing a robot, record that result accurately. If it also solved a recurring problem, the organisation has the beginning of a stronger business case.
